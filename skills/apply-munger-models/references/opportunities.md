# Opportunity review

Use for a startup, product wedge, acquisition or new line of business. Answer with evidence or mark unknown. Focus the final response on what can change the decision.

| Question | Evidence to seek | Inverted failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the problem consequential? | Frequency, cost, severity, workarounds and what happens if unsolved. | An interesting complaint does not justify switching or paying. |
| Whose problem and money? | User, buyer, approver, incumbent and each actor's incentives. | The user wants it but the buyer has no reason or budget to adopt it. |
| Why this wedge? | One workflow, concrete before-and-after outcome and initial distribution path. | The solution improves a step that is not the limiting constraint. |
| Why now? | Specific changed technology, rules, behavior or economics with dates and sources. | A vague trend is mistaken for a purchasing trigger. |
| What must be true operationally? | Data access, integrations, trust, error handling, support and procurement. | A dependency outside your control prevents the promised value. |
| Can it compound? | Repeat use, retention, learning from outcomes, economies or distribution advantage. | A demo advantage disappears as rivals copy the feature. |
| Can it make money? | Willingness to pay, acquisition cost, delivery cost, time-to-value and margins. | Value is real but capture is weak or service cost grows with volume. |
| What is the cost of this bet? | Capital, team time, delay to the best alternative and irreversible commitments. | It crowds out a better, nearer opportunity. |

## Two-sided causal argument

- **Forward:** If a specific user behavior or constraint is true, then the wedge changes a workflow outcome, causing adoption or payment and potentially a durable advantage. Identify the weakest causal link.
- **Invert:** If a plausible opposing fact is true, trace how it leads to resource loss or intolerable downside. Identify the earliest observable warning.
- **Discriminating test:** Seek one observation on which the stories predict different results. Prefer real task completion, repeated use, paid commitment, recovered value or switching behavior. Set the threshold before the test.

When evidence is thin, recommend bounded exploration rather than a sweeping go or no-go. Distinguish a good problem, useful product and attractive business.
